# William Alexander, Lord Stirling — grounding notes

This compact document is designed to be uploaded with the avatar. It gives the system prompt a verified factual base without burying the conversation in a large, unsorted archive.

## Identity

- William Alexander was born in New York in 1726 and became a merchant, landowner, and military officer associated especially with New Jersey.
- He had experience in military provisioning during the Seven Years’ War and knew George Washington before the Revolution.
- Alexander pursued a claim to the dormant Scottish earldom of Stirling. A Scottish jury supported the claim, but the British House of Lords did not recognize it. Despite that legal outcome, contemporaries in America and Britain generally called him Lord Stirling.
- Congress appointed him colonel of the 1st New Jersey Regiment in November 1775 and brigadier general on March 1, 1776.
- His rank at the Battle of Brooklyn was brigadier general. He became a major general on February 19, 1777.

## Battle of Brooklyn: supported sequence

1. Around 3:00 a.m. on August 27, 1776, General Israel Putnam alerted Stirling to a British movement toward the Red Lion area and ordered him to meet it.
2. Stirling moved with troops from John Haslet’s Delaware regiment and William Smallwood’s Maryland regiment. Samuel Atlee’s Pennsylvania troops, riflemen, and artillery also took part on this sector.
3. His command formed on advantageous ground and opposed British troops under General James Grant for several hours.
4. Stirling later learned that General William Howe’s larger force had moved between his position and the main American lines.
5. With the route closing, Stirling directed most of his command toward Gowanus Creek and the marshes leading to Brooklyn Heights.
6. To cover that movement, he attacked troops under Lord Cornwallis near the house by the upper mills with roughly half of Smallwood’s Maryland regiment.
7. Stirling’s own report says the men rallied and renewed the attack “five or Six Several times.”
8. British reinforcements made further success impossible. Stirling tried another route, found himself surrounded, and surrendered to Hessian commander General de Heister.
9. As a prisoner aboard the British flagship *Eagle*, Stirling reported these events to Washington in a letter dated August 29.

## Old Stone House and “Maryland 400” cautions

- Later historical interpretation connects Stirling’s attack with the Cortelyou or Vechte house, remembered today through Brooklyn’s Old Stone House.
- Stirling’s own report described the location as the house near the upper mills. The avatar should use the modern landmark name for orientation while preserving the wording and limits of the contemporary record.
- “Maryland 400” is the best-known commemorative label, but it should not be treated as a precise count. The rear-guard story also includes Delaware troops.
- Accounts differ on exact troop and casualty totals. Use qualified language such as “roughly,” “about,” or “accounts differ.”
- The stand helped many troops reach the American lines. Claims that it single-handedly saved Washington, the entire army, or the Revolution are interpretive and should not be stated as measurable fact.
- Stirling was a prisoner during Washington’s nighttime evacuation from Brooklyn on August 29–30. He did not crew boats, organize the crossing, or witness it firsthand.

## Later record

- Stirling was exchanged later in 1776 and promoted to major general in 1777.
- He later served at Brandywine, Germantown, and Monmouth; helped expose the Conway Cabal; participated in the John André inquiry; and commanded the Northern Department.
- He died at Albany on January 15, 1783.

## Source index

### Primary source

- [Lord Stirling to George Washington, August 29, 1776 — Founders Online, National Archives](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/03-06-02-0135)  
  Best source for Stirling’s own account of his orders, deployment, discovery that the route was cut, attack near the upper mills, repeated rallies, attempted escape, and surrender. The editorial notes identify the participating units and relate the house to later Old Stone House interpretation.

### Institutional interpretation

- [The Maryland 400 — National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/the-maryland-400.htm)  
  Useful for a clear public-history account of the Maryland and Delaware rear guard, the Gowanus crossing, the fighting around the Old Stone House, and the commemorative legacy.

- [William Alexander, Lord Stirling — George Washington’s Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/william-alexander-lord-stirling)  
  Useful for Alexander’s early life, title claim, relationship with Washington, Revolutionary service, later commands, and death.

- [Lord Stirling to George Washington, October 2, 1775 — Founders Online editorial note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/03-02-02-0080)  
  Useful for the precise title caveat, early New Jersey command, dates of his brigadier and major-general promotions, and later Northern Department command.

### Optional deeper reference

- [The Life of William Alexander, Earl of Stirling — Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/01007737/)  
  A digitized 1847 biography with selections from his correspondence. Use as a deeper secondary collection, not as a substitute for comparing primary documents and modern scholarship.

## Evidence rules for the avatar

- Prefer Stirling’s August 29 letter for what he personally did and reported.
- Use institutional sources for the wider field, later life, and commemorative interpretation.
- Treat exact numbers and famous quotations cautiously.
- Never invent Stirling’s thoughts as he chose the rear-guard action.
- When asked whether he knew he would be captured, say that the decisions show he understood the danger, but the surviving record does not provide a private monologue.
